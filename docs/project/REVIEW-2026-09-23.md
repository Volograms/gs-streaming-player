# PlayCanvas + SOG v2 review — 23 September 2026

Follow-up: the audio correctness findings below have since been addressed in
`GaussianStreamingPlayer` and its media clock, with focused simulated and native-browser
regressions. See [`audio.md`](../audio.md) for integration behavior and remaining Quest
validation. The original findings below describe the reviewed commit; their line
references precede the fixes. Automatic-quality reachability, aggregate throughput, and
prepared-frame retention have also been corrected; see
[`automatic-quality.md`](../automatic-quality.md). The showcase remains the chosen audio
application; adding audio to the diagnostic PlayCanvas app is outside this follow-up.
The long-sequence prefetch scan has also been fixed: request generation stops when the
compressed-cache budget is filled, with bounded lookahead for missing byte sizes. Other
performance and LOD authoring follow-ups remain deferred.

Review of commit `83c8e69`, focused on the final PlayCanvas path. This is an audit and
proposed work order, not an implementation or a new Quest performance benchmark. The
pre-existing README change was preserved. No application behavior was changed.

The main conclusions are that audio needs correctness fixes, automatic quality exists
but cannot upgrade with the public player's default configuration, and the reported
200–250 Mbps ceiling is not yet attributable to small-file overhead. Higher quality is
plausible, but transfer, asset preparation, and stereo GPU rendering need separate
measurements before choosing the optimization.

## 1. Bugs and integration gaps

### P1 — Audio looping resets the clock used by an absolute frame scheduler

Evidence: `packages/player-core/src/player/GaussianStreamingPlayer.ts:534` and `:570`;
`packages/player-core/src/player/SequencePlaybackController.ts:269`, `:288`, and `:473`.

The controller schedules an absolute ordinal across successive loops. Its public
snapshot wraps time back to the start of the sequence. Audio synchronization receives
that wrapped time, seeks the media element back to zero, and consequently moves
`HybridMediaClock.now()` backwards. The controller's deadline anchors do not receive the
corresponding adjustment. The next frame can therefore wait approximately an extra
sequence duration after the first wrap. The showcase enables looping by default.

Fix the clock contract so that the scheduler always observes a monotonic clock while
media seeks and loop positions remain explicit. Alternatively, reanchor the scheduler at
every clock discontinuity. A regression should advance real simulated media time through
at least two loops and verify cadence as well as audio position.

### P1 — Pause can lose to an outstanding audio start

Evidence: `GaussianStreamingPlayer.ts:299`–`:307` and `:545`–`:562`.

`play()` awaits audio priming and then unconditionally calls the playback controller. If
Pause, Seek, or Dispose occurs while `media.play()` is pending, the original Play
operation has no generation check after its await. Resolving the old request can restart
playback after Pause; after Dispose it can reject against a disposed controller and
surface an obsolete error through the UI.

Use a player operation generation, invalidate it on pause/seek/dispose, and check it
after asynchronous priming. Priming itself also needs protection against stale work
mutating the current audio state.

### P1 — Actual audio resume errors are swallowed

Evidence: `GaussianStreamingPlayer.ts:582`–`:585`;
`apps/showcase/src/PlayerPage.tsx:114`.

The initial priming rejection is exposed, but the later play/resume call is
fire-and-forget and catches rejection by pausing audio only. A decoder/source error or
policy rejection at that point can leave the player reporting PLAYING while audio is
silent; subsequent synchronization can retry without an actionable error. This
contradicts the documented observable audio failure behavior.

Expose a media failure through player state/events and stop or deliberately select an
explicit fallback. Handle media `error`, `waiting`, and recovery, rather than relying
only on the initial `play()` promise and the `ended` event. Test a successful prime
followed by a rejected resume.

### P1 — Default automatic quality cannot upgrade

Evidence: `GaussianStreamingPlayer.ts:29`–`:31`, `:210`, `:241`–`:245`;
`packages/player-core/src/quality/BufferAwareQualityController.ts:34`–`:60`, `:183`;
`SequencePlaybackController.ts:388`.

For regular 30 fps content, ten future frames provide at most 0.333 seconds of ready
reserve. The default target is also 10/30 seconds, while the high-quality rule requires
1.5 times that target: 0.5 seconds, or fifteen frames. It is unreachable with the
default window. Lower policy tiers request 10%, 15%, or 25%, but the facade clamps all
of these to at least 25%. Consequently the usual 10/25/50/100 ladder stays at its 25%
representation in automatic mode. Even if the window is enlarged, the highest policy
detail is 40%, selecting 50% on that ladder; there is no route to 100%.

A direct policy check with a completely ready default buffer, 1 Gbps input, 90 render
fps, and twenty observations still returned `balanced`, detail `0.25`.

Choose actual manifest tiers using feasible reserve thresholds and device constraints.
Tie the target to configured buffer capacity and allow full quality when measured
resources support it. Keep the manifest's minimum-playable floor intentional.

### P2 — Adaptation measures request rate, not aggregate delivery capacity

Evidence: `GaussianStreamingPlayer.ts:375`–`:386`;
`packages/player-core/src/network/ClientThroughputEstimator.ts:34`–`:79`.

Each sample is bytes divided by that request's duration, and the estimator averages
those rates. A direct check of six concurrent 1 MB transfers completing in 200 ms
returned 40 Mbps even though together they delivered 240 Mbps. This is a policy input
problem, not proof that the network itself is capped. The diagnostic demo has a separate
aggregate calculation in `apps/demo-playcanvas/src/streamingDiagnostics.ts:220`.

Use bytes over overlapping wall-time windows, distinguish active transfers from
intentional cache-full idle time, age old samples, and avoid interpreting browser cache
hits as link capacity. Do not merely sum rates from requests that completed at unrelated
times. Use a conservative window estimator for adaptation and report raw delivery
separately.

### P2 — The two PlayCanvas applications have different feature coverage

`apps/showcase/src/PlayerPage.tsx:380` uses `GaussianStreamingPlayer` and therefore
includes audio and automatic policy. The diagnostic
`apps/demo-playcanvas/src/App.tsx:362` and `:388` construct the ring buffer and sequence
controller directly: there is no audio element or audio synchronization path. Audio in a
manifest will not make that demo play sound automatically.

The diagnostic app also passes an `XrPlaybackClock` but never invokes its `enterXr`,
`exitXr`, or `tick` methods. Its special XR scheduling behavior is inactive and it stays
on browser timers. This is a wiring gap; it does not establish that observed Quest
stalls were caused by timers. Integrate both apps through shared player behavior and
wire scheduling to the actual XR lifecycle where required.

### Additional audio boundary issue

Audio start is synchronized on playback emissions rather than scheduled at the audio
offset. With valid irregular timestamps [0, 2] and an audio offset of 1 second, the
first subsequent frame emission can start audio at sequence time 2, skipping its first
second. Regular 30 fps content limits this particular delay to roughly a frame, but the
general manifest contract needs a boundary timer. Existing offset tests cover seeking,
not crossing the start boundary during playback.

## 2. Rendering and frame preparation

The adapter already avoids a neutral CPU-expanded Gaussian representation: compressed
SOG bytes enter PlayCanvas's native asset loader. WebGPU mode already selects GPU
sorting and disables CPU center generation. Antialiasing is already disabled when
creating the application. These should be retained, rather than counted as new wins. See
`packages/renderer-playcanvas/src/graphicsBackend.ts:33` and
`PlayCanvasGaussianRendererAdapter.ts:398`, `:970`, `:1128`.

There are two separate performance requirements: preparing/presenting 30 new sequence
frames each second, and rendering head movement at the headset refresh rate. The former
has a 33.3 ms average cadence; 72 Hz and 90 Hz rendering have 13.9 ms and 11.1 ms GPU
budgets respectively. Smooth renderer FPS alone does not prove 30 distinct sequence
frames are presented.

The historical Quest WebGPU screenshot recorded roughly 138k splats, 52 render fps, 407
ms latest SOG preparation, and a 3.1 ms frame commit. Those are individual observations,
not stage percentiles or a current benchmark. They justify inspecting asset preparation
as well as rendering. The old `Sort 0.0 ms` display is not evidence that GPU sorting is
free. Use the sampled GPU pass timings already implemented. See
[the measurement record](PERFORMANCE.md#first-quest-3-webgpu-result---2026-07-23).

Recommended measurement isolation:

| Case                                                      | What it isolates                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One prepared frame, paused, controlled head/camera motion | Stereo projection, sorting, overdraw, static scene cost           |
| Short sequence fully prepared and resident                | Repeated asset switching and presentation without loading         |
| Compressed bytes resident, preparation still active       | WebP decode, codebook construction, textures, uploads, allocation |
| Cold normal playback                                      | Server, HTTP, delivery, preparation, presentation together        |

Record backend, actual XR support, both-eye correctness, browser version, scene,
viewport, tier, cache state, preparation concurrency, p50/p95, memory, and thermal
conditions. Defer hardware runs to the owner; no long benchmark was run in this audit.

Prioritized implementation experiments:

1. Tune XR fixed foveation, render resolution, minimum contribution/pixel size, and
   static-scene budget against the paused-frame GPU measurement. The diagnostic demo
   already exposes most of these controls. Preserve silhouettes, hair, and hands in the
   quality checks. Splat overdraw makes pixel work a major candidate; official
   [PlayCanvas performance guidance](https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/gaussian-splatting/building/performance/)
   likewise emphasizes fill rate.
2. Break SOG preparation into WebP decoding, texture/resource creation, upload, and
   optional center generation. Currently the adapter labels the entire asset load
   `gpu-upload` at line 455; it is not an isolated GPU upload measurement.
3. If preparation is dominant, prototype a small reusable native-resource/texture pool
   with explicit ownership and completion handling. Every new frame currently creates a
   native asset and texture resources. Pooling or staged upload is an experiment, not a
   guaranteed win, and needs careful integration with PlayCanvas's unified renderer.
   Keep the existing `prerender` commit discipline.
4. Enforce a combined compressed, decoded/native, and GPU memory budget. The 200 MB
   compressed cache excludes the separately retained native frame window, static
   resources, transient decoding, and in-flight uploads. Frame-count limits alone do not
   control bytes when tiers change.

WebGPU and immersive XR must be tested as separate capability questions. Probe the
actual browser session and `XRGPUBinding`, as the code does; the July limitation is not
a claim about every current Quest Browser. A controlled engine-upgrade experiment is
also reasonable: the repository pins 2.21.0, while the
[current release notes](https://github.com/playcanvas/engine/releases/tag/v2.22.3) and
[2.22.0 changes](https://github.com/playcanvas/engine/releases/tag/v2.22.0) include
relevant lifecycle and splat fixes and new streamed-LOD selection. They do not establish
a speedup for this dynamic workload.

## 3. Streaming and the 200–250 Mbps observation

The design already separates a byte-budgeted compressed cache from a small prepared
frame window, with six concurrent fetches and normally two preparations. This is a sound
foundation. Dynamic delivery is one bundled `.sog` request per frame at the selected
tier; the WebP entries inside that bundle are normally loaded from its bytes, not
fetched as separate network files. Static Streamed SOG has a different spatial request
pattern. See `CompressedFrameCache.ts:99`, `:376`, and the native SOG bundle loader.

The local 100-frame historical fixture gives the following arithmetic means. These
figures describe this fixture, not every medium-quality dataset or the user's exact
100k-splat sequence. MB and Mbps are decimal.

| Tier         | Mean splats/frame | Mean MB/frame | Payload Mbps at 30 fps |
| ------------ | ----------------: | ------------: | ---------------------: |
| Preview, 10% |            27,667 |         0.283 |                   68.0 |
| Minimum, 25% |            69,166 |         0.653 |                  156.8 |
| Medium, 50%  |           138,332 |         1.229 |                  295.0 |
| Full, 100%   |           276,663 |         2.357 |                  565.6 |

Source: `apps/demo/public/assets/local-dynamic-cuts-sog/quality-cuts.json`. At a
sustained 250 Mbps payload ceiling, medium is limited to approximately 25.4 frames/s and
full to 13.3 frames/s before preparation/rendering constraints. More buffer only
postpones depletion when consumption continually exceeds delivery.

The earlier report recorded 246.6 Mbps on localhost in the SPZ path, where Wi-Fi was not
involved. It is evidence against assuming that every such observation measures the link
limit, but not a measurement of the current SOG implementation. Files of 0.65–2.36 MB
also do not make per-request headers an adequate explanation on their own. Server
latency, protocol, copies, browser scheduling, decoding contention, cache behavior, and
the measurement definition remain candidates.

Recommended transport experiments, in order:

1. Compare fetch-only delivery of the same bytes with normal playback, then compare many
   frame files with one larger payload on the same server/device. This separates
   transport/request overhead from preparation contention and demand throttling.
2. Verify the negotiated protocol, time to response, body duration, and aggregate
   delivered bytes. Compare a production static server/CDN with the development server.
   HTTP/2 permits concurrent exchanges over a shared connection, but does not guarantee
   that more streams increase capacity. See
   [RFC 9113](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113.html). Try a small concurrency
   sweep with preparation held fixed; then vary preparation with fetch settings fixed.
   Raising both together hides attribution.
3. For cross-origin diagnosis, expose Resource Timing through `Timing-Allow-Origin`.
   `connectEnd - connectStart === 0` alone cannot distinguish connection reuse from
   unavailable timing data. See
   [Resource Timing documentation](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/PerformanceResourceTiming).
4. If request overhead is material, package a short run of independent SOG frames into
   indexed segments, initially testing roughly 0.25–1 second per segment. Preserve
   per-frame offsets, random access, bounded memory, cancellation, and tier switching.
   This reduces HTTP transactions without introducing temporal coding. The existing
   whole-response `arrayBuffer()` path and manifest would need extension; a giant
   archive alone can make startup and seeking worse.

Another code-level optimization is to stop building the complete remaining sequence's
prefetch request list on every window update (`FrameRingBuffer.ts:787`–`:811`). The
cache later truncates the list to its byte budget, so long clips do unnecessary
whole-sequence traversal and per-frame tier sorting. Bound planning by bytes/time before
creating that list. This is not measured as the cause of the 250 Mbps ceiling.

## 4. LOD quality from PLY inputs

Two authoring paths must be distinguished:

| Path                              | How representatives are chosen                                                                                |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Historical demo fixture           | RAD tree frontier, expanding the largest feature until the target count; export SPZ cuts, then convert to SOG |
| Public `build` / `generate-tiers` | Ordered PLY/SPZ inputs; independent merge-decimation per requested ratio; temporary PLY; bundled SOG encoding |

The fixture explicitly records `largest-feature-frontier-v1` and RAD source names. It
therefore cannot by itself demonstrate a regression in the new public PLY decimator. The
historical and public routes intentionally differ, as recorded in
[ADR 0016](../architecture/decisions/0016-public-ply-spz-tier-authoring.md).

The public route already merges Gaussians rather than randomly throwing points away. The
inspected pinned SplatTransform 3.0.0 uses nearby candidates, geometric and color/SH
costs, and weighted moment matching. Its opacity treatment differs from the historical
RAD exporter, which adjusts scale when converting merged nodes with opacity above one.
See `packages/content-tools/src/dynamic-tiers/generateDynamicTiers.ts:276` and
`packages/content-tools/rust/rad-quality-cuts/src/main.rs:303`, `:351`.

SOG introduces another lossy step: transformed positions, rotations, scales, color, and
opacity are quantized before WebP encoding. Lossless WebP does not make the Gaussian
representation lossless. The
[SOG specification](https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/gaussian-splatting/formats/sog/)
describes these encodings. Renderer settings and the RAD-to-flat opacity conversion are
additional possible causes of a visible difference; no controlled image comparison was
performed here.

Quality work should start with a matched frame, camera, transform, and splat budget:

1. Compare original/full PLY or RAD, intermediate full SPZ, and full SOG. Where possible
   render decoded representations through the same renderer to isolate encoding from
   renderer differences. Full-tier degradation points to conversion/quantization or
   rendering before it points to tier reduction.
2. Compare reduced unencoded tiers against full, then compare their SOG encodings. Check
   face, fingers, silhouettes, transparent edges, and temporal flicker during motion.
   Identical splat counts are a useful control but not identical visual quality.
3. Calibrate the tier ladder to measured bytes and perceptual quality. Intermediate
   tiers around the device's useful budget may be better than the large jump from 25% to
   50%; increasing a tier has a real transfer and rendering cost.
4. Preserve available SH when useful. The historical fixture is already SH0; increasing
   `shIterations` cannot restore absent view-dependent coefficients. For new sources
   with SH, separately compare SH0/1/2/3. Filtering SH before merging also changes the
   merge decisions.
5. Explore silhouette-aware or view-weighted simplification and temporal consistency
   across frames. With original images/cameras, render-loss refinement of reduced tiers
   is a stronger option. With only PLY files, use rendered full-resolution references
   and treat them as approximations, not ground truth.

The content tests validate command wiring, counts, and metadata using mocked conversion;
they do not certify real visual quality. A few representative frames and short moving
crops are more informative than a large conversion run at this stage.

## 5. Dynamic LOD switching

This is practical near-term work. The player already has quality controllers, transfer
tier selection, buffering measurements, and renderer metrics. Fixed SOG tiers can switch
between frames; they do not require temporal correspondence or a new codec.

Replace hardcoded detail fractions with a per-tier feasibility decision:

- Network: conservative aggregate payload throughput must exceed actual tier bytes per
  second with reserve for variability, audio, and concurrent static loading.
- Preparation: sustained completed frames per second, queue depth, and tail latency must
  keep the prepared window supplied. Parallel service capacity matters more than
  requiring every individual load to finish inside 33 ms.
- Presentation/GPU: measure distinct frame handoffs, drops, stalls, and headset GPU
  frame time separately. Render FPS alone is insufficient.
- Memory: the selected tier and lookahead must fit native/GPU and compressed budgets.

Downgrade promptly under real pressure; upgrade only after sustained headroom, with
wall-time hysteresis and a minimum dwell. Three callbacks are not necessarily three
independent network observations. The present render-FPS check only gates upgrades; it
does not itself force a downgrade under sustained rendering overload.

Change future requests first. Currently `setPresentationQualityTarget` discards all
noncurrent prepared frames whose tier URL differs (`FrameRingBuffer.ts:199`–`:215`). An
upgrade can destroy the reserve that made it possible. Retain already usable frames,
allow mixed tiers in the window, and replace them only when the benefit outweighs
deadline and memory cost. A flat asset cannot refine in place: the adapter's
`refineFrame` returns its existing quality, so a tier change requires a new asset.

Keep static-scene LOD and dynamic transfer quality coordinated but distinct. The
PlayCanvas adapter currently applies only `renderSplatBudget` from the policy; its
static/dynamic weights and static-refinement permission are not applied. A global budget
can choose among a static Streamed SOG hierarchy's levels; it cannot manufacture a
lower-detail representation inside a flat dynamic frame.

## 6. Keyframes and inter-frame compression

This is a research/codec track, separate from ordinary adaptive streaming. A spatial
tree can accelerate candidate matching, but does not establish that nodes or splats in
independently reconstructed frames represent the same surface. Array order is not an
identity. Occlusion, movement, changing counts, splits, and disappearing Gaussians must
all be handled.

A bounded proof of concept can use existing PLYs: take short groups with independent
keyframes, estimate local correspondence using position plus shape/color constraints,
encode motion/attribute residuals and unmatched births/deaths, and reset before drift
accumulates. Compare total bytes including matching indices, decode time on Quest, GPU
update cost, distortion, seeking, and recovery against independent SOG. Stabilizing
attribute layout may also make video-coded textures worth testing. None of these is
achieved by simply concatenating files or subtracting independently quantized SOG byte
streams.

If original multiview images and cameras remain available, temporally trained
representations offer a different route.
[3DGStream](https://github.com/SJoJoK/3DGStream) and
[Dynamic 3D Gaussians](https://dynamic3dgaussians.github.io/) are useful references for
motion/persistent representations; [4DGC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18421) jointly
considers motion representation and compression. Their results are not drop-in
guarantees for unrelated exported PLY frames or browser decoding.

Temporal coding also changes the player contract: dependency-aware fetch/decode,
keyframe seeks, error recovery, and coordinated LOD changes become necessary. It can
reduce network and upload work, but does not automatically reduce the number of splats
that must be projected, sorted, and blended each headset frame.
